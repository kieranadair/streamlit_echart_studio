# Research and teaching decisions

These are the sources behind the course design. Each entry connects an observed teaching practice to a choice an author can make. The six five-day sections, lesson timing, and assessment criteria are our proposals. [Course charter](README.md) · [Daily sequence](syllabus.md)

## Rachel Thomas and learning the whole game

**Start with an activity worth getting good at.** In *Providing a Good Education in Deep Learning*, Thomas identifies two influences. David Perkins describes teaching a manageable version of a whole activity, then practising its difficult parts and using it in new settings. Paul Lockhart asks what mathematics education loses when it teaches notation before people experience the pleasure of posing a problem or explaining an idea. Thomas also cites Ken Robinson on making room for mistakes. Her practical commitments include accessible tools, concrete examples, room for creativity, and revisiting technical ideas at greater depth. Give learners the monthly revenue question first. Let their need to identify a missed target create a reason to learn `series.encode`. [Thomas’s essay](https://rachel.fast.ai/posts/2016-10-08-teaching-philosophy/) · [Perkins at Harvard Project Zero](https://pz.harvard.edu/resources/making-learning-whole-how-seven-principles-of-teaching-can-transform-education) · [Lockhart’s essay](https://math.jhu.edu/~brown/Documents/LockhartsLament.pdf)

**Return to a mechanism in a new situation.** Thomas’s computational linear algebra course uses singular value decomposition in topic modelling, background removal, and regression. Its repeated appearances give learners reasons to investigate how it works. Our equivalent is `datasetIndex`. See a single source on day 2, an explicit index after a filter on day 8, and a separate fitted table on day 18. By day 20, tracing the index answers an important question about which observations a claim describes. [Course notebooks and sequence](https://github.com/fastai/numerical-linear-algebra)

**Let learners formulate questions.** Thomas describes succeeding at assigned problems while needing practice choosing and scoping her own. An open choice between two colours provides little practice at that skill. An invitation to decide whether a regional manager needs totals, ranks, or channel mix changes the appropriate view. By the final section, the learner writes the question and identifies evidence that could overturn the answer. [Thomas on asking questions](https://rachel.fast.ai/posts/2016-10-08-teaching-philosophy/)

## Jeremy Howard, SolveIt, and the feedback loop

Howard’s practical deep learning course puts a complete working example in the opening minutes and pairs it with executable notebooks. His public guide to George Pólya describes understanding a problem, planning an approach, trying it, and looking back. His separate SolveIt reference gist describes the mechanics of a dialog with executable code, editable notes, and prompts. The first is a source for the questioning method. The second explains the workspace in which someone can practise it. [Fast.ai lesson 1](https://course.fast.ai/Lessons/lesson1.html) · [Howard’s Pólya gist](https://gist.github.com/jph00/d60301884c56fe063101a7cc6193b3af) · [Howard’s SolveIt reference gist](https://gist.github.com/jph00/9e7b444aba5ecf6d14295ba2cee890c3)

A **read–evaluate–print loop (REPL)** lets a person act on a small piece of a problem, immediately see the result, use it as evidence, and try a revision while the context is still in mind. A fast redraw alone can reward random clicking. A prediction makes an unexpected result informative. An inspectable intermediate value helps the learner locate the cause. A second attempt tests whether the explanation travels. For this course the sequence is **predict → edit one thing → observe the chart → inspect the option or rows → revise and retest**. Studio reruns the app and keeps the editable chart document in session state. This is a teaching analogy to a REPL. Learners use the existing controls to run the experiment. [Howard’s introduction to dialog engineering](https://www.fast.ai/posts/2024-11-07-solveit.html) · [SolveIt reference](https://gist.github.com/jph00/9e7b444aba5ecf6d14295ba2cee890c3)

The **16 November 2026** fast.ai public-launch article is by Howard **and Rachel Thomas**. Jack Hogan’s colour-palette example interleaves a visual result, the property he is trying to achieve, and the calculation. Howard describes reimplementing ideas while reading a paper. The authors identify Bret Victor among their influences. Victor’s *Learnable Programming* explains why seeing the state between input and output matters. Our translation is a learner who can point at a region, its transformed row, and the option entry that encodes it. On day 26 they read a small gallery example alongside a reconstruction. Optional AI help can ask for a prediction or point to one branch. All core exercises can be completed in Studio with the references. [Fast.ai public launch](https://www.fast.ai/posts/2026-11-16-solveit-launch/) · [Victor on making program state visible](https://worrydream.com/LearnableProgramming/)

| **Establish the mental model.** | The Apache Handbook separates data provision from visual mapping. Its transform chapter shows source and derived datasets and how series consume them. Have learners trace a real row before introducing implicit mapping, array indices, or styling. [Dataset and encode](https://echarts.apache.org/handbook/en/concepts/dataset/) · [Data transforms](https://echarts.apache.org/handbook/en/concepts/data-transform/) |
| **Study a small example, then consult the contract.** | Apache’s *Bar with Background* supplies category and series arrays. Reproduce its bar tracks using the Studio DataFrame. [Live example](https://echarts.apache.org/examples/en/editor.html?c=bar-background) · [Example source](https://github.com/apache/echarts-examples/blob/88ca004030e999073a15303e5fe32462b8fefae2/public/examples/ts/bar-background.ts) · [Bar series reference](https://echarts.apache.org/en/option.html#series-bar.showBackground) · [Native Streamlit API](https://docs.streamlit.io/develop/api-reference/charts/st.echarts_chart) |
| **Reason about what an interaction does.** | The University of Washington curriculum uses editable notebooks to move from encodings and transforms into linked views. Its interaction chapter distinguishes the user’s input, the selected records, and the effect of the selection. Use the same question on day 22. A region can be highlighted, outside a zoom window, or absent from the transformed dataset. Each demands a different check. [Curriculum](https://idl.uw.edu/visualization-curriculum/) · [Interaction notebook](https://idl.uw.edu/visualization-curriculum/altair-interaction) |
| **Evaluate the audience’s task separately from rendering correctness.** | Tamara Munzner’s nested model distinguishes the domain problem, the abstraction of data and tasks, visual encoding and interaction, and algorithmic implementation. A correctly rendered and sorted chart can still rank the wrong measure. The capstone requires both a correct option and an answer to a reader’s question. [Munzner’s model and validation paper](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/labs/imager/tr/2009/NestedModel/) |

## A reason to fade the hints

Atkinson, Renkl, and Merrill studied fading worked steps together with prompts to explain their underlying principle. Their two experiments found transfer benefits. This is evidence from a different task and population. Our testable design choice is to give control-level hints on early days, branch-level hints later, and a reader brief at the end. On checkpoints, ask learners first to reconstruct from memory, then let them use the official reference as they would at work. In the pilot, record whether a learner can handle one *changed* case. [Study abstract](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ678596) · [Course pilot](assessment.md)

Schwartz and Bransford offer a reason to time an explanation carefully. Learners who have already compared two cases can bring a more specific question to the explanation. Our ranked-bar exercise puts this into practice. Two views have the *same* top-to-bottom ranking while their first plotted rows differ. After the learner discovers that, sorting and axis inversion are two ideas they have a reason to distinguish. [Primary study](https://aaalab.stanford.edu/papers/time_for_telling.pdf) · [Three Studio experiments](contrasting-cases.md)
